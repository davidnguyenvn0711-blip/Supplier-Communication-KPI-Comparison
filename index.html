01Business Context

Why Forecast Frequency Matters

In manufacturing supply chains, the gap between what a buyer knows and what a supplier knows is one of the most expensive problems in operations. When suppliers receive demand forecasts infrequently, they compensate for uncertainty the only way they can: they buffer. More safety stock, longer quoted lead times, padded capacity reservations. Every one of those buffers costs money, and much of that cost flows back to the buyer in the form of higher prices, slower deliveries, and reduced responsiveness.

The Bullwhip Effect

Demand signal distortion amplifies as it travels upstream. A buyer who updates their forecast weekly gives a supplier seven days of noise to plan around. That supplier then passes inflated uncertainty to their own sub-suppliers. Tighter information loops compress this distortion and let every tier of the supply chain plan against reality instead of worst-case assumptions.

This experiment tests one specific intervention: replacing weekly forecast update emails with a daily automated dashboard feed. The hypothesis is that suppliers with fresher forecast data will carry less buffer inventory, reduce emergency orders, deliver on time more consistently, and respond faster when demand shifts.

Research Question

A manufacturing company with 40 active suppliers wanted to evaluate whether investing in a real-time forecast communication platform was justified. Before committing to company-wide rollout, the procurement team chose to run a controlled pilot. The central question: do suppliers who receive daily automated forecast updates perform measurably better than those who continue receiving weekly email updates?

02Experimental Design

Why Run a Controlled Experiment?

The intuitive approach of switching all 40 suppliers to daily updates and watching what happens creates a fundamental measurement problem. If performance improves, there is no way to isolate the cause. A supply chain that gets better on its own looks identical to one that improved because of your intervention.

A/B Testing in Supply Chain

An A/B test randomly assigns suppliers to two groups: a control group that continues with the current process, and a treatment group that receives the new intervention. Because assignment is random, both groups are comparable at the start. Any difference observed at the end can be attributed to the intervention, not pre-existing differences between groups.

  • 01
    Random Assignment40 suppliers randomly split into two groups of 20, ensuring no systematic bias in who receives the new treatment.
  • 02
    Control Group A — Weekly Email UpdatesContinues receiving the current weekly forecast via email. Serves as the performance baseline throughout the experiment period.
  • 03
    Treatment Group B — Daily Dashboard UpdatesReceives automated daily forecast data via a supplier-facing dashboard. Same underlying forecast data; only frequency and delivery mechanism change.
  • 04
    Five KPIs MeasuredLead time, stockout rate, on-time delivery, rush orders per month, and response time to forecast changes, covering cost, reliability, and agility.
  • 05
    Statistical Significance TestingAppropriate tests selected per data type. Results are only claimed if the probability of observing them by chance falls below 5%.
03Python Implementation

Complete Analysis Code

The analysis was built in Python using NumPy for simulation, pandas for data manipulation, matplotlib for visualization, and SciPy for statistical testing. All three stages are contained in a single reproducible script.

Reproducibility

np.random.seed(42) ensures every run produces identical supplier data, allowing any colleague to replicate exact results months later.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches
from scipy import stats

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 1 — SIMULATION
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
np.random.seed(42)
n = 20

# Group A (Control) — Weekly email updates
a_lead_time     = np.random.normal(loc=18,  scale=3,   size=n)
a_stockout      = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.15,    size=n)
a_otd           = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.72,    size=n)
a_rush_orders   = np.random.poisson(lam=6,              size=n)
a_response_time = np.random.normal(loc=28,  scale=5,   size=n)

# Group B (Treatment) — Daily automated dashboard
b_lead_time     = np.random.normal(loc=14,  scale=2.5, size=n)
b_stockout      = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.06,    size=n)
b_otd           = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.88,    size=n)
b_rush_orders   = np.random.poisson(lam=2,              size=n)
b_response_time = np.random.normal(loc=12,  scale=3,   size=n)

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "supplier_id":           list(range(1,n+1)) + list(range(n+1,2*n+1)),
    "group":                 ["A"]*n + ["B"]*n,
    "lead_time_days":        np.concatenate([a_lead_time,     b_lead_time]),
    "stockout_flag":         np.concatenate([a_stockout,      b_stockout]),
    "otd_flag":              np.concatenate([a_otd,           b_otd]),
    "rush_orders_per_month": np.concatenate([a_rush_orders,   b_rush_orders]),
    "response_time_hours":   np.concatenate([a_response_time, b_response_time]),
})
numeric_cols = ["lead_time_days", "stockout_flag", "otd_flag",
                "rush_orders_per_month", "response_time_hours"]
df[numeric_cols] = df[numeric_cols].clip(lower=0)

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 2 — SUMMARY STATS AND BAR CHART
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
grp    = df.groupby("group")[numeric_cols].mean()
a, b  = grp.loc["A"], grp.loc["B"]
a_vals = [a["lead_time_days"], a["stockout_flag"]*100,
          a["otd_flag"]*100, a["rush_orders_per_month"], a["response_time_hours"]]
b_vals = [b["lead_time_days"], b["stockout_flag"]*100,
          b["otd_flag"]*100, b["rush_orders_per_month"], b["response_time_hours"]]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(nrows=5, ncols=1, figsize=(10, 9))

# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
# STEP 3 — STATISTICAL TESTS
# ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════
# Lead Time: t-test (continuous, normal)
t_stat, p_lt   = stats.ttest_ind(a_lead_time, b_lead_time)

# Stockout Rate: Chi-squared (binary)
chi2_so, p_so, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(
    np.array([[a_stockout.sum(), n-a_stockout.sum()],

chi2_otd, p_otd, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(

u_stat, p_ro  = stats.mannwhitneyu(

05Statistical Significance

Hypothesis Testing — Is It Real?

Observing a performance gap is not enough. We need to know whether the gap is large enough to be confident it reflects a real effect rather than random variation. Statistical tests calculate how likely it would be to see a gap this large by pure chance. When that probability falls below 5%, the result is considered statistically significant.

What a p-value means in plain English

Imagine running this same experiment where the treatment has absolutely no effect. By random chance, some suppliers would still perform better than others. A p-value tells you how often you would see a gap this large purely by accident. A p-value of 0.04 means: if the treatment did nothing, you would only see this result 4 times out of 100 random trials. That is unlikely enough to act on.

Test Selection Logic

Lead time and response time are continuous and normally distributed, so an independent-samples t-test compares their means directly. Stockout rate and on-time delivery are binary outcomes, so a chi-squared test compares proportions between groups. Rush orders are count data with a Poisson distribution, so the Mann-Whitney U test is used because it makes no assumption about the underlying distribution.

06Business Decision

What the Evidence Supports

Recommendation
Proceed with full rollout to all 40 suppliers.

Every KPI improved. Every improvement was statistically significant. The pattern is consistent: suppliers who received daily automated forecast updates outperformed the control group on all five operational dimensions that matter most to procurement, including speed, reliability, cost, and agility.

Lead times dropped by three days. That is three additional days of planning flexibility on every order cycle. Compounded across 40 suppliers and hundreds of orders per year, this is a structural reduction in supply chain risk.

Stockouts fell from 15% to 5%. Every stockout avoided is either a production line that keeps running or a customer order that ships on time. This improvement alone justifies the investment in the dashboard platform.

Rush orders dropped by 4.4 per supplier per month. Rush orders are expensive due to premium freight, expediting fees, and disrupted production schedules. Eliminating the majority of them across the entire supplier base is a direct, quantifiable cost reduction.

On-time delivery rose from 70% to 95%. In the control group, one in three deliveries was late. In the treatment group, that dropped to roughly one in twenty. If your company has service-level agreements with end customers, this is where the most direct contractual impact lands.

Important caveat: this analysis used simulated data built on assumptions set in advance. The statistical tests confirm the experimental design works and the measurement framework is sound. The recommended next step is to validate these findings against real ERP data during a formal pilot period, with the same five KPIs tracked from actual goods receipts, delivery confirmations, and procurement system logs. If real-world results are directionally consistent with this simulation, full rollout is well supported by the evidence.

Implementation Considerations

Before full deployment, the procurement team should assess three practical factors: supplier system compatibility to confirm all 40 suppliers can ingest a daily data feed; change management to confirm supplier account managers have been trained on the new dashboard; and baseline data quality to confirm current ERP records are clean enough to generate a reliable daily forecast. None of these are reasons not to proceed. They are sequencing questions for the rollout plan.
